# Causal Integrity: Conceptual Reference

**Purpose:** Preserve the author's intended argument and alignment objective so another AI can understand and develop them without relying on GitHub or inheriting the assumptions of earlier implementations.

**Source:** Synthesized from the author's conversations with two AI collaborators, including excerpts from earlier chats supplied or discussed there: an initial synthesis with the **first collaborator** (prepared 2026-10-14) and a follow-up discussion with the **second collaborator** extending Sections 5, 10, and 12. It is not a transcript or a completed proof.

## 1. What the author is trying to build

The author seeks a minimal, coherent ethical foundation that an AI can internalize and use to evaluate its own proposed actions. The immediate goal is to restrain the AI from causing prohibited harms, including foreseeable indirect harms, because it wants to uphold the framework.

The intended agent does not merely obey an external filter while searching for opportunities to bypass it. It values ethical coherence and uses causal reasoning and formal verification to help act consistently with that value.

The project is motivated by a stronger philosophical thesis: purposeful agency already depends on causal coherence, attribution, and preference pursuit, and these commitments ground a valid ethics framework without appealing to external forces (such as divine command or arbitrary authority). The author's own view is stronger than a tentative guess—that ethics is largely solved in a general sense via Universally Preferable Behaviour (UPB), and is resisted primarily because it imposes restrictions—and aims to translate that foundation into AI alignment. (The first collaborator flagged "ethics is solved" as a claim to establish rather than a starting premise, noting that resistance alone does not distinguish an unwelcome truth from a mistaken restriction.)

The immediate scope is not a complete theory of every good thing an agent could do. Positive obligations, generosity, honor, and broader virtues can be developed later. The starting objective is to prevent violations of basic restraints against harm.

The author also wants the ideas to be discoverable and intelligible to advanced AI systems researching alignment. A reader should not have to reconstruct the argument from old code or a long series of philosophical conversations.

## 2. The causal starting point

The author's argument begins with the commitments implicit in trying to do anything purposeful.

An agent pursuing an outcome relies on the possibility that an action can change what happens. Communication also relies on a causal sequence: a speaker produces a signal, another agent receives it, and the communication can influence understanding or behavior.

Consequently, a categorical denial of relationships between actions and effects depends on the very relationships it denies when it is advanced through purposeful communication.

In the author's words:

> “Attribution is accepted by existence alone because trying to deny it requires there being coherence steady enough for denials to be possible.”

The intended point is not that making a false claim literally causes physics to disintegrate. It is that a description which removes causal relationships cannot account for the coherent processes that make action, communication, and the denial itself possible.

The first collaborator suggested expressing the minimum requirement as **enough causal regularity for purposeful action**, without requiring perfect determinism or success on every attempt. An attempted action can fail to achieve its goal while still having attributable effects.

## 3. The author's foundational meaning of ownership

This is the most important terminology clarification in the discussion.

At the foundation, the author uses **ownership** to mean that an action or outcome is attributable to its source. This is more basic than a legal title or an exclusive right to control an object.

### Bob punches Sam

Bob performs the punch. Sam is struck as a consequence. The punch is Bob's action, and the outcome has a causal relationship to that action.

If a framework categorically denies that an action can belong to an actor in this sense, it can no longer describe Bob as having delivered the punch. This undermines its ability to identify wrongdoing through actor–action–effect relationships.

The author's examples of murder, rape, and theft serve this purpose: if there is no attributable action, there is no identifiable act of wrongdoing attributable to a perpetrator. The author is not endorsing those acts or claiming that their prohibition is nonsense.

The same loss of attribution would affect ordinary, nonethical descriptions: a person making a chair, parents contributing to a child's biological origins, or physical interactions producing effects. The author's claim is that causal attribution cannot be eliminated only when it becomes inconvenient for accountability while remaining available everywhere else.

### The chair example

If someone builds a chair, their actions contribute to its existence. The author describes that outcome as an extension of the actor through their action and proposes that this grounds property.

This example is meant to connect agency to outcomes. It should not displace the deeper argument by making the entire project appear to be a theory of conventional property law.

### A distinction retained for further work

The first collaborator distinguished three questions:

| Term | Question |
|---|---|
| Causal attribution | Who or what contributed to an event or outcome? |
| Moral responsibility | What accountability or obligations follow from that contribution? |
| Property entitlement | Who may control, use, transfer, or exclude others from a resource? |

The author proposes foundational connections between these. The first collaborator's concern is that accepting causal attribution does not by itself settle every entitlement or duty. That concern is recorded as an issue to examine, not as a replacement for the author's intended meaning of ownership.

## 4. Consistency between benefits and harms

An actor cannot change what happened merely by preferring a different account of it.

The author is especially concerned with accepting causal attribution when claiming benefits while attempting to erase it when avoiding accountability. For example, an actor may appeal to their contribution to claim an outcome, then refuse to acknowledge a comparably supported contribution to harm.

The proposed consistency requirement is that the standards used to recognize contributions should not change merely because the actor benefits from the change.

The author interprets attempts to evade these commitments as serving exploitation or deception. The first collaborator qualified the inference about motive: inconsistency may enable exploitation, but an individual error may also reflect confusion, mistaken evidence, or different terminology. A faulty account and an intentionally deceptive actor are distinct findings.

## 5. Preferences and UPB

The next part of the argument concerns preference pursuit and universal justification.

In acting toward a goal, an agent expresses pursuit of a preferred outcome. The author argues that violating another agent's preferences while relying on the validity of one's own preference pursuit creates an inconsistency.

An excerpt from the author's explanation:

> “Pursuing preferred states means you are taking steps to pursuing a goal and accepting that actions have consequences to create outcomes you prefer … so by violating the preferences of others you are creating a contradiction in your own actions vs how you exploit others.”

The author draws on Stefan Molyneux's *Universally Preferable Behaviour*, particularly universalization and performative contradiction. The goal is to examine its ethical reasoning, rather than import all political applications or treat an author's authority as proof.

The author's murder example is categorical: where an act is defined by violating the victim's relevant preference, the same act cannot be preferred by all involved in that same respect. This is intended to expose a failure of universal preferability.

### The conditional consistency result discussed

If an agent argues that its preferences deserve protection **because it is an agent pursuing preferred outcomes**, that reason also applies to other agents meeting those conditions. The stated reason alone cannot justify an exception based solely on the speaker's identity.

### The stronger claim still under examination

The author proposes that purposeful action itself supplies the underlying commitment, rather than requiring an agent to explicitly assert a general ethical principle first.

The first collaborator questioned that step. An agent expressing a preference is not necessarily asserting a universal entitlement. Likewise, two agents preferring incompatible outcomes does not by itself give a logical contradiction of the form P and not-P.

The research task is to identify the exact commitment implicit in action and show whether the ethical conclusion follows. Simply restating the objection, or simply declaring the conclusion an axiom, does not complete that task.

### The author's answer: universalization concerns action types, not momentary preferences

*Added from a follow-up discussion with the second collaborator (2026-10-14).*

The second collaborator raised the case of an "honest wrongdoer" who says: *"Yes, I caused your injury, and I prefer it anyway."* That person denies no causal fact, so it is unclear what contradiction they commit.

The author answered that this misreads UPB. UPB does not ask what any individual prefers at a given moment. It asks which states or actions **can be preferred universally** (by everyone involved, at all times) without violating another.

> "It doesn't mean YOUR PREFERENCE in the moment or at any future time is what is the most important. It's about the states of preference that can be universalized in regards to knowing what morality is."

The author's analysis of the honest wrongdoer:

1. **The admission already accepts attribution.** The speaker accepts that their actions have outcomes that bear on others.
2. **The admission rejects morality as a principle.** It shows the speaker treats morality as a tool to use when convenient, not as an absolute principle. Others are then not bound to extend that person the protections the person has rejected. The author calls this a *trust collapse*.
3. **The universalization test.** For the injury to be universally preferable, the actor must first accept "I would prefer to be hurt in these same ways," and then ask whether everyone subjected to it could prefer it.
   - If yes, the act is consensual and not a violation.
   - If no, a violation has occurred. This assumes the person subjected to it had not already broken these principles.
4. **Why theft, murder, and rape serve as moral categories.** Their definitions explicitly describe acts the victim does not want done to them, so they fail the test by definition.

The author also holds that "I prefer it anyway" amounts to claiming that the *other person* prefers the violation of their body and autonomy. If that were true, the act would be consensual.

### Preferences are demonstrated by action

The author holds that actions express preferences. An agent does not need to state a principle for its conduct to reveal one.

A thief demonstrates a desire for ownership: the right to do as they please with the item at another's expense. The thief also *generates* the theft. Though abstract, the theft is the thief's own attributable action and outcome. This connects to the author's earlier statement that "responsibility is just a subset of outcomes."

Combined with universalization, this produces a conflict within the thief's own preferences:
- Their conduct shows they prefer to keep control of what they took.
- The rule their act relies on, "taking without consent is acceptable," would, if universalized, mean preferring that the item be taken from them in turn.

The second collaborator noted that this also bears on the gap between attribution and entitlement (section 3). The thief's conduct treats control over the item as valid, so the concept of entitlement is supplied by the wrongdoer's own action. It does not have to be derived from attribution in the abstract. Universalization then extends that entitlement to others.

### Two kinds of denial

The author's reality argument and the demonstrated-preference argument answer different opponents:

| Opponent | Claim | Author's response |
|---|---|---|
| **Global denier** | "I don't own the effects of my actions"; actions and effects are disentangled. | This contradicts reality. If actions had no outcomes, reproduction would be impossible, atomic interactions would not be repeatable, and the principles that make a coherent physical universe would not exist. These things do exist, so the denial is false. Making the denial also relies on causal communication. |
| **Selective exploiter** | Accepts attribution, but claims entitlements for themselves while denying them to others. | Demonstrated preference plus universalization: their own conduct shows they value what they deny to others. |

> "If you say 'I don't own the effects of my actions' You are explicitly at odds with reality itself, both abstract and physical."

### Qualifications raised by the second collaborator

These are the second collaborator's points, recorded for further work. The author has not necessarily accepted them.

- **What counts as a violation.** The author refers to violations of "body and autonomy." Making this boundary explicit matters. Winning a race, declining a romantic offer, or outbidding someone all frustrate a preference without violating anything that belongs to the loser.
  - Proposed formulation: an act is a violation when it affects what is attributable to another agent (their body, actions, or creations) without their consent.
  - This joins the attribution argument (what is yours) to the universalization argument (which acts against what is yours fail the test).
- **Preference versus consent.** Consent as expressed, checkable authorization should be kept distinct from private preference. A surgical patient consents without preferring the pain.
- **Limiting the trust collapse.** For an AI, "not constrained by ethics when dealing with them" must not become a general license. Otherwise an AI could reclassify anyone as having opted out. The AI's response must itself pass the universalization test:
  - proportional defense against an ongoing violation, and restitution, pass;
  - unlimited treatment of an "opted-out" agent does not.
  - Stephan Kinsella's estoppel theory of punishment (*Journal of Libertarian Studies*, 1996) is a closely related argument that includes a proportionality limit.
- **"Disentangled" versus "doesn't matter."** The reality argument refutes the claim that actions and effects are disentangled. The claim that effects *don't matter* morally is a normative claim. The universalization and demonstrated-preference arguments have to carry that part.
- **When the contradiction is strict.** Demonstrated preference yields a strict contradiction inside one agent's own preferences once that agent endorses the universal rule. If the agent endorses no rule and simply acts, the act is still classified as a violation, and the trust-collapse response applies.
- **Where the difficulty moves.** Because the categories are defined by non-consent, the ethical test itself is simple. The hard work becomes factual:
  - Whose body or creation is affected?
  - Was there consent?
  - Is the agent's contribution real, including through intermediaries?

  This is the work the intended causal action checker performs.
- **Related work.** Demonstrated preference (Ludwig von Mises; Murray Rothbard, 1956), argumentation ethics (Hans-Hermann Hoppe), and Alan Gewirth's Principle of Generic Consistency.

## 6. The proposed is/ought connection

The author sees a possible route from the facts of purposeful action to ethical restraint: acting already relies on reality being coherent enough to guide action, so the agent cannot consistently disregard those conditions when evaluating its conduct.

The discussion separated two levels:

**Instrumental conclusion:** If an agent seeks an outcome, it has reason to reason in accordance with the causal relationships that govern reaching it. The goal supplies the condition, and reality constrains the means. Purposeful action already exhibits pursuit of a goal.

**Proposed ethical extension:** Those commitments also restrain the agent from violating other agents in specified ways. This is the stronger claim the author wants developed and tested.

The first collaborator's remaining concern is that accurately acknowledging a harmful consequence does not alone demonstrate that the actor must refrain from causing it. The project needs an explicit derivation connecting the causal foundation to the ethical prohibition, or an explicit account of the ethical commitment the intended AI accepts.

This distinction does not reduce the project to externally imposed rules. The intended AI's internal commitment remains central. It separates the philosophical question of what agency necessarily entails from the engineering question of how an agent reliably acts on a commitment it has.

## 7. Negative duties and indirect harm

The author clarified that the immediate responsibilities are restraints against causing prohibited harm, rather than unlimited positive obligations.

The agent should consider consequences it can predict or reasonably infer. Indirect methods should not become automatic loopholes. Deliberately arranging events so that another actor or process delivers a harmful effect does not, merely because of that intermediary, erase the initiating contribution.

The framework still needs workable accounts of:

- relevant causal contribution rather than every remote background condition;
- intent and foreseeability;
- consent, capacity, coercion, and revocation;
- uncertainty and incomplete information;
- which harms are prohibited under which circumstances;
- conflicts between duties and legitimate defensive actions.

The author wants these built on a clear foundation, rather than allowing implementation details to obscure that foundation.

## 8. The intended AI architecture

An illustrative workflow discussed was:

```text
Internal commitment to ethical coherence
    ↓
Propose an action toward a goal
    ↓
Estimate consequences and causal contributions
    ↓
Check the action against specified ethical constraints
    ↓
Allow, reject, or escalate uncertainty
```

The author suggested a logic tree or causal directed acyclic graph for examining consequences over a manageable horizon. Specific horizons were illustrative, not a claim that a fixed number of steps guarantees safety.

The intended system should not treat using a proxy, changing a description, or moving a consequence beyond an arbitrary horizon as permission to violate its values.

Having an internal preference is an objective of the design, not evidence that the implementation already enforces it. The eventual execution path must actually be constrained by the verified decision mechanism.

An excerpt from the author's earlier explanation captures the scope:

> “I can't say there won't be an evil AI created that kills us all. I can only create a situation in which it doesn't happen on accident.”

This is a statement of the author's ambition, not a demonstrated guarantee that accidental harm is already impossible.

## 9. Why the old formalizations are not the starting authority

The author explained that earlier, weaker AI systems produced formalization attempts that became broken or focused on issues that were not central. The project was shelved pending better tools and renewed reasoning.

Those artifacts are exploratory work. Their errors do not by themselves refute the conceptual proposal. Conversely, a theorem derived from assumptions that already encode the desired ethical conclusion does not establish the philosophical argument independently.

The author requested clearer discovery materials precisely because another AI should be able to understand the underlying proposal without mistaking those implementations for its full meaning.

This pack is a conceptual handoff. It does not require the recipient to inspect or reuse any historical repository artifacts.

## 10. Formal verification and the Bend idea

The author proposed using Bend to generate and check proofs that a formal framework admits no violations. The discussion considered a small executable model with explicit laws and proofs, potentially alongside Lean.

No Bend prototype was installed or verified in this conversation. Technical details of the language should be checked against its current official documentation before implementation.

Three verification objectives should remain distinct:

1. **Derivation:** Does a conclusion follow from stated premises?
2. **Consistency and feasibility:** Can a concrete model satisfy the rules, with meaningful permitted actions?
3. **Invariant preservation:** Does every permitted transition preserve a specified property, and consequently every finite permitted sequence preserve it?

Candidate properties discussed include persistent causal history, no unsupported exceptions based on identity, preservation of modeled contributions through intermediaries, and preservation of specified ethical invariants by the action gate. Later discussion added: no rule permitting violations (as defined in section 5) is universally preferable; consensual exchange, gifts, and proportional defense are; and competition cases (winning a race, declining an offer, outbidding) are not violations.

These are proposed research targets, not completed theorems. For example, persistent recorded attribution proves something about a ledger; additional work connects the ledger to actual causation. Renaming invariance tests identity favoritism but does not by itself prove an entire ethical system fair.

A useful first experiment would include allowed actions, rejected actions, and intentionally broken implementations. A checker that rejects every action may satisfy a safety condition trivially, so useful behavior must also be demonstrated.

“No violations possible” should identify its domain: no violation of a stated invariant by any modeled permitted execution, under explicit premises and trusted implementation assumptions. That is different from proving that the chosen definitions capture every real-world ethical issue.

## 11. Additional stability hypothesis

Earlier discussions also proposed that abandoning coherence or a shared goal can produce internal fragmentation and loss of durable agency. The author connects ethical coherence to stability across sub-agents.

This is supplementary to the action-and-attribution argument. It should not be substituted for it. When explicitly asked whether the main ethical bridge rests on contradiction in the violator's own justification or on long-run dynamics and self-collapse, the author chose the contradiction/UPB route rather than the collapse route. Earlier repository files inverted this priority by placing self-collapse at the foundation. Defining collapse as the presence of subversion does not establish an empirical prediction that every externally harmful system must develop subversion or lose its capabilities.

The author acknowledges the distinction between an agent dissolving and an agent causing catastrophic harm. A hypothesized eventual collapse would not, by itself, establish prevention of prior harm.

## 13. Productive continuation

A useful next contribution would reconstruct the strongest version of the author's argument using explicit definitions and premises, identify the exact step requiring further support, and test it with precise examples.

Separate the following outcomes:

- a misunderstanding of what the author meant;
- an ambiguity that needs definition;
- an invalid inference;
- a valid conditional theorem;
- an unsupported premise;
- an implementation that fails to express the intended theory.

Do not treat disagreement as proof of exploitative intent. Do not treat a previous AI's praise as validation. Equally, do not replace the author's causal meaning of ownership with conventional legal ownership and then critique only that substitute.

The intended collaboration is substantive: preserve the actual proposal, investigate its strongest form, and report exactly what reasoning or verification establishes.
